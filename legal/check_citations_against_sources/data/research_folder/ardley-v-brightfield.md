# Ardley v. Brightfield Supply Co.

214 Westm. 2d 377 (2019)
Westmark Supreme Court

## Facts

Erik Ardley worked for Brightfield Supply Co. for six years as a regional sales manager. His territory was Dunmore, Pell and Ashby counties. When he was promoted he signed a covenant not to sell competing products anywhere in Westmark for two years after leaving Brightfield. In 2017 he resigned and joined Castellan Industrial, a competitor, as a sales representative for the same three counties.

Brightfield sued to enforce the covenant and moved for a preliminary injunction. In the alternative, Brightfield asked the trial court to rewrite the covenant so that it barred Ardley from all industrial sales in the three counties for one year. The trial court refused to enforce the covenant as written and declined to rewrite it. The Court of Appeals affirmed, and we granted review.

## Majority

Westmark has long viewed restraints on a person's ability to earn a living with suspicion, but it has never held them void as a class. Our cases weigh the employer's need for protection against the hardship to the employee and the public's interest in competition. We restate the rule today. A covenant not to compete is enforceable only to the extent it is reasonable in duration and geographic scope and necessary to protect a legitimate business interest such as customer goodwill or confidential information.

The burden of showing that a covenant meets that standard rests on the employer that seeks to enforce it. An interest in avoiding ordinary competition is not a legitimate business interest. Brightfield's interest lay in the customer relationships Ardley built at its expense in three counties. A ban on selling anywhere in Westmark protected far more than that. It kept Ardley out of thirty-six counties where he had no customers and no knowledge of Brightfield's accounts. The covenant was broader than necessary, and the trial court was right not to enforce it as written.

Brightfield argues that the trial court should then have rewritten the covenant. We disagree. When a covenant is broader than necessary, a trial court may narrow it: it may enforce the covenant against a smaller territory or for a shorter time than the covenant states, if the narrowed restraint is one the covenant's own terms contain. A trial court may narrow but not rewrite such a covenant. It may not add terms, extend a restraint, or substitute a different restraint for the one the parties chose. Brightfield's proposed bar on all industrial sales, a restraint the covenant never mentioned, would have been a new contract written by the court.

Nor was the trial court required to narrow the covenant on its own motion. Narrowing is an equitable remedy, and a court may decline it where the employer drafted a covenant it had reason to know was overbroad. The trial court found that Brightfield used the same statewide form for every employee from warehouse clerks to vice presidents. It did not abuse its discretion.

Affirmed.
